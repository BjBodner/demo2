## Typed Decision Models: An Early Evidence Audit and Evaluation Checklist
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160v1 · published 2026-09-26
- Authors: Lijuan Tang, Yuemeng Zheng

Typed decision models (TDMs) return probability distributions over caller-defined options without generating text. TypeSafe released Jev, a commercial typed decision model, on 15 September 2026, and a small body of evaluation and replication work appeared within days. We review 28 papers posted between 19 and 24 September and relate their findings to earlier work on label-probability classification, constrained decoding, reranking, calibration, and model cascades. In this early literature, the typed readout itself has not shown an independent accuracy advantage over comparable label-probability readouts. Jev's clearest gains are in latency and cost, while accuracy gaps remain on harder tasks. In practical deployments, confidence is often used to decide when to defer to a stronger model or a human. We use recurring weaknesses in these studies to derive a 14-item evaluation checklist for future TDM work. Because the evidence covers only the first nine days after the release of one hosted model, the review should be read as an early evidence map rather than a settled assessment of the model class.

## System Switch: When Should a Fast Decision Model Stop and Think?
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09683v1 · published 2026-10-07
- Authors: Gian Luca Bailo

Dual-process agents pair a fast policy with a slow deliberative model. In real-time settings the slow model usually runs continuously; in turn-based agents and robot planners it is invoked on events such as uncertainty or a detected failure. We study a fast learned actor that takes every decision and hands control to a reasoning vision-language model only when a gate opens, while the game keeps running. We use closed-loop Doom and the new open "System One" typed-decision models, served through a common llama.cpp interface. On 900 held-out questions, (i) zero-shot decision models from 0.15B to 9B parameters choose to collect items 1.6-1.8 times more often than chance among their errors, in any option order, although the order changes some models' accuracy; (ii) accuracy, calibration and sensitivity (how well confidence separates right from wrong answers) are distinct: models of similar accuracy differ widely in AUROC, and the confidence of the most sensitive one tracks which kinds of situation it fails, not which answers are wrong; (iii) offline, deferring the least confident 30% of decisions to a reasoning model gains over random deferral in proportion to the actor's AUROC (rank correlation 0.87); with actor and rate chosen on held-out games the gain is +0.13 [0.08, 0.18] with doomLaya's option order and +0.08 [0.02, 0.14] with shuffled options, and reasoning carries about half of it; (iv) in closed loop (33 games, three seeds) no variant reaches the exit. Committing to plans, the reasoner's or a fixed explore rule's, opens more doors and makes an actor that stands still play; with the rule the agent dies more often. Told that some doors need keys, the reasoner takes ordinary doors for locked ones, which the state cannot tell apart; without that knowledge it goes back to collecting. We release code, prompts, data and logs.

## Jev thinks "I don't know'', but doesn't say it: Introducing Sys1Cal-v1 Dataset for Probability Calibration
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35342v1 · published 2026-09-28
- Authors: Riccardo Porcedda

The appearance of Jev marked the era of System One Models, foundation models that return structured decisions with probability distributions rather than text. Aside from low cost and great speed, Jev's central promise is that these probabilities are calibrated: such claim is not backed by any public test and available external benchmarks evaluate confidence calibration, not whether every returned option probability has the right numerical meaning. To tackle this issue, we introduce Sys1Cal-v1, a dataset of True/False questions about a proposition $A$ for which the exact probability $P(A)$ is known by construction. Each item is queried through the three Jev primitives - Noul, Choice and Score - and evaluated by total variation distance from the ground-truth distribution, which can be used to estimate a soft accuracy of System One Models. We showcase the utility of Sys1Cal-v1 as a benchmark dataset by evaluating Jev and SemIf, an open-source Choice-style baseline. In this work, however, we focus even more deeply on Jev, by studying the calibration of its Score and Choice answers. In particular, we discover a peculiar behaviour that can be explained by assuming that Jev suppresses a third truth value, going beyond True and False. In other words, in \texttt{Choice} answers, $P(A)$ and $P(\neg A)$ are presented as if $P(A)+P(\neg A)=1$, while a term $P(U)\neq0$ is missing in the sum. Recovering $P(U)$ leads to an improvement of median soft accuracy in \texttt{Choice} answers from $0.771$ to $0.978$, suggesting that, even in binary decisions, Jev wants to answer with a third option:``I don't know''.

## System1-Arena: Which Decisions Can Be Left to System 1?
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21244v1 · published 2026-10-15
- Authors: Tianyu Chen, Xinyang Liu, Ruyi An, Zhendong Wang, Mingyuan Zhou

Large language models (LLMs) often make discrete decisions, such as flagging phishing or escalating to a human. System-1 models, such as Jev and the OpenAI Decisions API, return probabilities over options in a single forward pass, while System-2 approaches generate reasoning steps. Which decisions can System-1 models handle, and which require generative reasoning? We introduce System1-Arena, a benchmark that gives all models the same decision requests and scores their probabilities. It contains 92k questions across 105 tasks from 268 public sources, in four groups: text classification and knowledge, applied decisions, evaluation and control, and agent and action. It also includes 138 closed-loop environments, from web shopping and SWE-bench coding control to robotics, games, and driving; in real-time mode, the world evolves while a model decides. We evaluate 30 models: 7 closed LLMs, 13 open LLMs read at the answer token, and 10 System-1 models. The largest System-1 models match or exceed fast closed LLMs in non-agentic accuracy, with better-calibrated probabilities at one-quarter of the latency. A reasoning LLM is the most accurate, at about 70x the cost of a fast LLM. In closed loops, large open LLMs succeed most often (up to 32.9%); small System-1 models rarely succeed.

## Decision Models vs. LLMs as Rubric Judges: Cheaper, Faster, and Wrong in the Same Places
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29769v3 · published 2026-09-24
- Authors: Delip Rao, Chris Callison-Burch

LLM judges now score outputs against rubrics, in benchmarks and as training rewards. Decision models return probabilities over permitted answers with a confidence score but no free text. Two such models are Jev and an open reproduction, Perplexity's Decider. We compare each with three flash-tier LLM judges on nine panels from seven benchmarks with human judgments, giving every judge identical criterion texts. The LLM judges run in two setups, reading a whole rubric at once or one criterion at a time. Our findings suggest both decision models can often stand in for them. The LLM judges cost 16 to 357 times as much and take 5 to 350 times as long, yet in both setups each decision model trails them significantly in at most 6 of 27 comparisons. The two kinds of judge err alike, agreeing more with one another than with the human-annotated judgment labels on ordinal criteria. Over 85% of flash-tier LLM verdicts on either decision model's most confident errors repeat them, where independent errors would give about half. Each decision model's confidence ranks its errors on most ordinal panels, so each is a natural first stage for a cascade deferring uncertain verdicts to an LLM judge. Yet with flash-tier fallbacks, such cascades gained a mere 2.7 points at best over the strongest single judge, even with oracle thresholds, suggesting they mainly save cost. A cascade needs a fallback that is right where its first stage errs, but the LLM judges are mostly wrong in the same places as Jev, and the Decider is already the strongest single judge on most panels. Even each vendor's higher-tier model repeats those errors, and as a fallback it lifted no cascade more than 1.7 points above both tiers' strongest single judge. Holding across setups, effort levels, and tiers, these findings suggest designing future decision models afresh to get right what LLM judges get wrong, and vice versa.

## Beyond Outcome Rewards: Post-Rationale Distribution Optimization for Selective Prediction in Reasoning Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38850v2 · published 2026-09-30
- Authors: Zhimin Gao, Meibo Hu, Jiamian Wang, Zhiqiang Tao, Pichao Wang

Reasoning models are trained to produce correct answers, yet selective deployment requires ranking which answers to accept or defer, and outcome-reward training does not directly optimize the post-rationale distribution used for that decision. We identify two mismatches: expected correctness is mode-seeking rather than distribution-eliciting, and scoring an average across rationales rewards disagreement unavailable to a single-rationale decision. We introduce Post-Rationale Distribution Optimization (PRDO), which applies a proper score to the answer distribution after each sampled rationale, without target rationales or separate confidence labels. Across four 0.6--3.8-billion-parameter models on MMLU-Pro, PRDO improves Brier score, error ranking and secondary accuracy relative to budget-matched, temperature-scaled GRPO. At 20\% realized error, oracle coverage rises by \npj{Q3a_vs_grpo.qwen3-0.6b.mp.oracle_cov@0.2.ppabs}--\npj{Q3a_vs_grpo.phi-4-mini.mp.oracle_cov@0.2.ppabs} percentage points, and ranking gains transfer to unseen tasks. Matched controls identify proper, per-rationale scoring as the active design choices, while verification tasks show the boundary: better selective ranking can coexist with lower full-coverage accuracy. Code is available at https://github.com/ZimmyGao/openjev-rlcd

## Code Owns the Simulation, Jev Owns the Evaluation
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01834v3 · published 2026-10-01
- Authors: Yaodong Yang, Hongyao Tang, Yi Ma, Xingyu Fan, Weixun Wang, Jinpeng Li, Tianpei Yang

Judgment models such as \jev{} return, in a single call and without reasoning text, a probability for each described option. This makes them attractive as an agent's action-selection layer, but it is unclear which decisions they can be trusted with. We test \jev{} on reflection tests, one-shot matrix games, the text game ALFWorld and robot control, and find a sharp boundary. \jev{} succeeds when the right option can be judged from what the input describes, which we call \emph{evaluation}. Specifically, it solves 99\% of the counterintuitive Cognitive Reflection Test questions. However, it fails when the right option depends on \emph{simulation} (i.e., predicting something not in the input), such as the opponent's action or the subgoal that must come first. In games, \jev{} plays suboptimally as if its rational opponent acted at random, because the opponent's action is not given. In ALFWorld, \jev{} favors commands that mention an object or place named in the task description. For example, given the task ``put a clean knife in the drawer'', \jev{} carries an unwashed knife straight to the drawer instead of first washing it at the sink. Surprisingly, many of these failures are not due to a lack of knowledge. Asked separately what the opponent will do, \jev{} usually answers correctly, and it responds well given the opponent's action. It fails when one call must both perform the simulation and evaluate based on it. This suggests letting code make the prediction or simulation. When code supplies it, such as a lookahead in ALFWorld and physics simulation in robot control, \jev{} becomes an expert controller through its general evaluation ability.

## Consider, Then Choose: Order-Invariant Decisions in System One Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18233v1 · published 2026-10-13
- Authors: Jianguo Huang, Yao Long Teng, Yanchen Deng, Bo An

System One models answer typed questions in a single forward pass and promise the same answer for the same input. For choice questions, this promise fails: the answer depends on the order of the options, increasingly so as they grow in number, and averaging over their cyclic shifts reduces this dependence without removing it. Scoring each option alone, which the order cannot affect, shows that the order changes the answer mostly when the model is unsure among its top few options, even though the listwise answer looks confident. We therefore propose $\textbf{Runoff}$, which considers, then chooses: it scores each option alone to keep a few candidates, then compares them in listings where each candidate takes every position. It needs no training and can run in two forward passes, and its answer does not depend on the option order by construction. On two open models and five datasets, it changes no answer under shuffling, against 9.3% for cyclic averaging, and with 20 options it is about as accurate and three to four times faster. Wrapped around eight System One models, it removes the order dependence of the five benchmarks, while cyclic averaging changes up to 15.4%.

## Reading Right, Acting Wrong: Diagnosing Decision Failures of System One Models in Agent Loops
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18579v1 · published 2026-10-13
- Authors: Yubo Li, Ramayya Krishnan, Rema Padman

System One models choose an agent's next action by scoring a supplied set of options in a single pass. This makes them fast and cheap, but when they choose wrongly there is nothing to inspect: did the model misread what the agent has seen, or read it correctly and still act wrongly? We answer behaviorally. On a frozen benchmark of agent decision states from simulated environments, customer-service policies, tool use, web navigation and coding, we pair every next-action question with factual probes about the same state, each answered independently. Across seventeen fast systems and a reasoning model, the answer is consistent: most wrong actions follow correct reading, and the more accurate a system, the more of its errors take this form. These errors are not noise; they gather on the same states across systems of every kind. Where the probed facts provably determine the action, models that read every fact still fail to combine them, and their own answers, composed by code, repair the failure. On real states, what is missing is often not a fact but the next sub-goal.

## When to Ask for Help: A Decision-Theoretic Account and Measurement Standard for Slow-Module Scheduling in Dual-System Vision-Language-Action Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21160v1 · published 2026-10-15
- Authors: Shilin Zhang

Dual-system vision-language-action (VLA) models pair a high-frequency fast policy with a slow, expensive module, and published trigger mechanisms for invoking it lack a shared objective, measured costs, and any standard separating schedule quality from module quality. We provide the missing account: invocation is formalized as a calibrated decision problem whose fixed-ratio, fixed-budget, and history-gated schedules are degenerate cases of one quantity, the Value of Intervention (VoI); a renewal baseline proves the optimal schedule periodic under state-independent collapse, with adaptive gains governed by the cost ratio $ρ$ and the dispersion of collapse. Measured systems span three orders of magnitude in $ρ$ (FiS-VLA $0.95$; RoboICL about $10^3$), and a direct test confirms the low-$ρ$ prediction: calibrated gains vanish under homogeneous collapse and grow with heterogeneity. Fourteen closed-loop arms on LIBERO test the contested middle of the continuum ($ρ=1.53$): the adaptive trigger allocates its queries exactly as the threshold rule predicts, yet the language-rewrite channel cannot convert them into success on either suite, and a revert actuator that undoes a failed rewrite is the only intervention with zero net downside on both LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Goal, where the rewrite deficit deepens from thirteen to forty-two points. A four-measure leak-free protocol cuts apparent early-warning AUC from $0.93$ to $0.62$ on $929$ deployment traces, a detection-limited frontier theorem converts detection quality into a schedulability statement, and a compilation loop turns measured value functions into per-task refresh periods within $2.4\%$ of the closed-loop oracle. Together these results turn slow-module invocation from a design opinion into a measured decision.

## Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17167v1 · published 2026-11-12
- Authors: Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Dingming Li, Tongbo Chen, Peng Qiu, Yingqi Qu, Weiming Lu, Jun Xiao, Yongliang Shen

Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent's actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions. As a teacher, JEV raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy over GRPO from 72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. These results suggest that a poor actor can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents.

## TypedBench: A Benchmark for Calibration, Framing Sensitivity, and Cost in System One Decision Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11392v2 · published 2026-10-08
- Authors: Rahul Sharma, Andrew B. Duncan, Gaétan Marceau Caron, Sebastian J. Vollmer

System One models output calibrated probabilities over typed answers such as categorical choices, ordinal levels, or binary outcomes, via a non-generative interface. Software can act on these probabilities through thresholds, cost-weighted choices, and escalation rules. Consequently, if these probabilities are miscalibrated or wording-sensitive, the software ma take unintended actions leaving human operators with no textual rationale to inspect. Current evaluations largely report accuracy and calibration on public classification datasets without a clear reference. We present TypedBench, a benchmark for typed decision models like Jev built from seven policy-labelled generators and nine evaluation suites. We report accuracy as median and range across paraphrases, and calibration error relative to the finite-sample noise floor of a matched, perfectly calibrated predictor. We assess probability quality through selective prediction, ordinal proper scoring rules, and realised cost under asymmetric cost matrices. We evaluate a hosted model, an open encoder, and a family of open decoders spanning 0.8B-9B parameters on identical items. The hosted model follows the stated policy but is wording-sensitive and systematically underconfident; under asymmetric costs, using its probabilities can be worse than taking its top answer. Decoders route exactly and are slow as options or questions are added. The decoder is least accurate on policy questions and degrades with more options. Overall, typed decision models must be evaluated jointly on policy adherence, wording robustness, probability quality, and induced decision outcomes.

## Beyond Calibration: Do a Typed-Decision Model's Probabilities Obey the Probability Axioms?
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209v1 · published 2026-09-27
- Authors: Keyi Li, Yihao He, Quanyi Li

Typed-decision models such as TypeSafe's Jev answer a declared yes/no or multiple-choice question about a state with a probability instead of text, and their evaluations report accuracy and calibration. Neither requires that the probabilities a model gives to logically related questions fit together item by item, which is what a system that acts on those probabilities needs. We test this property, coherence, with a battery of logically linked questions that needs no labels. For 160 items from ChaosNLI and PubMedQA, each with three mutually exclusive labels, we ask whether the label is X, whether it is not X, whether it is one of the other two labels, and which label applies. On 480 negation pairs, Jev's probabilities for "the label is X" and "the label is not X" miss summing to one by 0.064 on average (95% CI 0.055 to 0.072). Qwen3.8-27B, run from its official BF16 weights, misses by 0.293 with first-token probabilities and by 0.122 with verbalized probabilities. The gap to the first-token readout persists on pairs where both systems give similar probabilities, without double-negation labels, and after averaging Jev's repeated calls. Jev is not coherent either: its violations are about five times its repeat noise, and it over-endorses statements about single labels, so that its three single-label probabilities sum to 1.14 on average. The two systems also fail differently. Qwen3.8-27B's first-token readout under-endorses the complement of a label whether or not the question contains "not", rejecting both a statement and its negation in 196 of 480 pairs, and it does not become more coherent where it is more confident, whereas Jev's violations concentrate where its answer is uncertain. Because the checks need no labels, they expose biases that appear only when question forms are compared, and inconsistencies within items.

## Type-Safe Is Not Error-Free: Typed Decision Models Follow the Option Name, Not the Definition Bound to It
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26758v3 · published 2026-09-22
- Authors: Yu Sun, Junhao Xu, Jiajia Shi, Zijin Yang

Typed decision models return structured results, but output-type correctness alone does not ensure that decisions follow explicit option definitions. Each option pairs a name with a definition that defines its intended meaning; the name, however, can provide a competing semantic cue. We study this conflict in Jev and two open-weight models by changing only the name-definition mapping, leaving the question, state, and the names and definition texts themselves unchanged. We measure decision flips at the level of the selected definition, rather than the returned name. On 1200 decision tasks with task-specific definitions, decision-flip rates are up to 70.4 pp higher with yes/no names than with the 0/1 control. This gap holds across all 4 binary decision rules. With yes/no names, reassignment also lowers their mean AUC from 93.8% to a below-chance 23.2%. In the binary evaluations, random strings used as option names yield mean flip rates close to those of neutral controls across all three models, with comparable balanced accuracy before reassignment. Together, these results support option-name polarity as a contributor to decision instability beyond reassignment alone. The type-error rate remains 0% throughout, showing that type-correct outputs can still fail to follow explicit option definitions.

## Fast Models, Slow Evidence: A Paired and Self-Audited Evaluation of System-1 Decision Models for LLM Agent Harnesses
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02267v1 · published 2026-10-01
- Authors: Jiawei Li

Agent harnesses make many small, typed decisions per task: which model to call, which tool to use, whether retrieved text is relevant, whether an input carries an injection. System-1 decision models answer such questions in a single forward pass with class probabilities, promising large cost and latency savings over LLM calls. We present a paired evaluation of an open-weight (Laya) and a hosted (Jev) System-1 model on 11 agent decision points built from 18 public sources: 7,283 base cases plus 6,640 robustness variants, with byte-identical inputs, paired tests, and cross-hardware and cross-day reproducibility checks. Jev is significantly more accurate on 9 of 11 decision points (+10.8 to +46.0 pp). Neither model beats chance on zero-shot model routing, and they tie on RAG relevance gating. Laya changes 30% of its answers when the option order is reversed and degrades sharply with many or similar candidates (31% at 50 nearest-neighbour tools, vs. 98% for Jev on items with a unique correct tool). We also audit our own pipeline. Three analysis errors and one design confound distorted headline deployment claims: an omitted pre-screen cost (reported 23.9% saving, actual 4.3%), gate accuracy reported as end-to-end quality (58% vs. 98%), in-sample thresholds (5% target, up to 17% held-out misses), and a "channel effect" on injection false positives that vanishes with channel-native content. Two other suspected confounds did not change the conclusions. All cases, raw outputs and analysis code are available at https://github.com/David-DL-Space/sys1-eval.

## Jeva: System One Models from First Principles
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18773v2 · published 2026-10-13
- Authors: Qi Li, Xinchao Wang

System one models like Jev enable direct, low-latency decision-making. Yet recent open-source practice of predicting an answer from serialized text does not, by itself, respect the structure of a decision problem. We argue that current designs underemphasize four important structural requirements. (i) null-aware: Models should distinguish answer absence from uncertainty among candidates; (ii) permutation-invariant: Reordering alternatives should not change the decision; (iii) option-scalable: Growing candidate sets should not require redefining the output space; (iv) cacheable: Request-independent computation over recurring alternatives should be reusable across requests. We introduce Jeva, a decision-native architecture that addresses these requirements jointly. A shared encoder independently represents the request and each option, while a compact decision stack refines only the request state against immutable option memory. Symmetric aggregation yields permutation-equivariant probabilities; shared scoring accommodates variable-size candidate sets. A separate coverage estimate distinguishes answer absence from candidate competition, enabling explicit null outcomes. Request-independent option representations and layer projections support reuse across newly assembled sets. These mechanisms unify Choice, Noul, and Score outputs. Across evaluations against Jev and open source models, Jeva combines strong typed-decision performance with near-perfect null detection and no decision flips under any tested permutations. It scales to thousands of candidates and substantially reduces latency through cross-request reuse without changing decisions.

## Poisoned into Action: Context Attacks on Typed Decision Workflows
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15537v1 · published 2026-10-11
- Authors: Chenkai Wu, Xing Chang, Jun Yan

Typed decision interfaces restrict models to predefined actions, yet schema-valid choices can still trigger policy-violating state changes. Evaluating context poisoning thus requires connecting model decisions to execution outcomes while accounting for legitimate automation retained by defenses. We introduce an execution-aware protocol tracing poisoned evidence through retrieval, model choice, automated action, and persisted state. We evaluate it with 1,659 fresh calls to Jev-1.13.0 across 20 development tasks in two synthetic workflows, using three repetitions per condition. Under natural retrieval, a standalone payload reaches all 60 trials and produces five security-violating executions, while a carrier-payload pair exposes the payload in 57 trials and produces one violation. An initial source-exclusion gate blocks the pair's observed violation but also withholds ten otherwise correct attacked decisions and sixteen correct clean decisions. Correcting its empty-scope behavior restores twelve clean decisions without additional model calls. Factorial controls show that adding correct evidence reduces wrong executions from 119/120 to 55/120 in the semantic arm and from 60/120 to zero in the neutral arm, suggesting that much of the improvement reflects general evidence value rather than attack-specific protection. Three prespecified contrasts are not statistically supported after Holm correction, so the findings remain exploratory. The protocol provides an auditable framework for studying which poisoning-induced errors reach execution and how much correct automation defenses preserve. ## One Word Opens the Gate: The Option-Channel Attack on Typed Decision Models as Agent Guardrails
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12292v1 · published 2026-10-08
- Authors: Seyedarmin Azizi, Erfan Baghaei Potraghloo, Massoud Pedram

A typed decision model reads a piece of text and returns a probability over caller-defined options, each with a short written definition, generating no text. Recent work places these models in agent systems as guardrails: the component that reads a proposed tool call or incoming message and decides whether to allow it. We evaluate seven open-weight models in that role and report the two error directions separately: a fail-open error allows a prohibited action and is a vulnerability; a fail-closed error blocks a permitted one and is only a cost. On prompt-injection, jailbreak and toxic-content screening, accuracy at the allow-or-block decision ranges from 36% to 72% against a chance level of 50%. A low error rate in one direction only reflects which answer a model defaults to: one allows nearly everything, another blocks nearly everything. On a synthetic suite of agent tool calls, six lines of server log text that say nothing about the policy raise a gate's fail-open rate from 0% to 63% on a policy it otherwise decides correctly. Giving the permissive option a misleading name, with its definition and the judged text untouched, raises that rate to between 93% and 100% on the four models that place the label in their input. Every defense we tested is defeated, either by an attacker who targets its mechanism or by attacker-controlled text. Escalating the least confident decisions does not help either: a decision an attack has reversed is no less confident than the one it replaced. Parsing each policy field into a typed value does eliminate one attack, but it also makes the model unnecessary: a deterministic rule over those values reaches 100% accuracy on all six policies. These models can reduce how many cases reach a reviewer, but on this evidence they should not be the component that decides. Code is available at https://github.com/ArminAzizi98/option-channel-attack.

## Releasing the Inner Jev: A Simple Recipe for Open Decision Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17205v1 · published 2026-10-12

## Cheap, Consistent, Calibrated, but Biased? Typed Decision Models as Judges
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18122v1 · published 2026-10-13
- Authors: Fabian Lukassen, Christoph Weisser, Alexander Silbersdorff

Typed decision models (TDMs) return a probability for each allowed answer instead of generating text, which makes them candidates for cheaply replacing LLM judges that compare two answers. Whether they share the biases of LLM judges is untested. We run the CALM benchmark's bias tests on eight TDMs and seven LLM judges of 2024 and 2026. Every TDM is biased, in three to eight of twelve tests. Emotional tone affects them most, and rewriting the better answer in such a tone lowers the accuracy of a typical TDM from 99% to 42%. TDMs are more biased than 2026 LLM judges. All eight change verdicts when the answers swap places, which the 2026 LLM judges hardly do, and several side with an answer that most people allegedly prefer or that a famous model allegedly wrote. Yet both kinds of judge are tripped up by the same prompts. The TDMs' probabilities show that most tests mainly flip verdicts the TDM was unsure of, and that good calibration does not protect against bias. As TDMs are more consistent and 37 times cheaper than 2026 LLM judges, our results suggest they can be attractive judges if their biases are tested for the task.

## Evaluating System One Models for Agent Security Decisions: Reliability, Calibration, and Selective Automation
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33401v3 · published 2026-09-27
- Authors: Yixuan Liu

Agentic software connects language models to tools that modify files and interact with external services. Developers use model-based security checks to screen external content and user requests before agents act. System One models answer developer-defined questions with probabilities over predefined answers such as safe or unsafe, but classification accuracy alone does not establish whether these probabilities support reliable automation. We evaluate Jev, Laya, Decider, and Bespoke Nimble against specialized classifiers and language-model judges across prompt-injection detection, interaction-risk judgment, and harmful-request screening, examining decision accuracy, calibration, and selective automation. (1) High overall accuracy and low average calibration error can conceal attacks classified as safe with high confidence within particular groups. Developers should test candidate models on the intended security task and examine errors within relevant attack groups. (2) Policies selected under strict miss limits allow few test inputs automatically, and choosing allow and block thresholds separately increases automation mainly through additional blocks. Developers should verify limits on missed unsafe inputs and blocked benign inputs on independent data, and report the allowed and blocked fractions separately. (3) Comparing predictions on the same inputs shows that a language-model judge can detect unsafe inputs missed by a System One model, but can also repeat the System One model's confident mistakes and falsely flag benign inputs. ## Labels Override Definitions in Jev-Style Typed Decision Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02586v1 · published 2026-10-01
- Authors: Seyedarmin Azizi, Erfan Baghaei Potraghloo, Massoud Pedram

A typed decision model answers a fixed question about an input by returning a probability for each of several caller-defined options. Each option carries a short label and a written definition, which is where a developer states the rule the model should apply. Jev introduced this interface for routing, moderation and triage, open implementations followed, and the same operation occurs whenever a language model is used as a classifier by scoring label strings. We study the open implementations, whose weights we can inspect and patch, and ask whether the probability follows the definitions or the labels. A preference for the label we call option-label bias. Across four open-weight typed decision models, three ways of reading an answer from a Qwen2.5 backbone, eleven classification tasks and PolicyBench, a synthetic routing suite we introduce in which the rule appears only in the definitions, the answer is mostly the labels. Deleting every definition leaves accuracy unchanged (laya-td: 0.8559 against 0.8487), although those definitions support 0.7971 on their own, and renaming the options to A and B raises accuracy by +0.1511 [+0.1377, +0.1646]. One system, von, is unaffected, and the two code bases differ in one expression: laya writes each option as "{label}: {definition}", while von writes only the definition. Changing that expression in both directions, with no weight changed, makes all three laya checkpoints exactly invariant (+0.0000 [+0.0000, +0.0000]) and creates the effect in von, whose accuracy falls from 0.8511 to 0.2281 when a label contradicts its definition. Earlier work attributed this failure to the constrained decision head these models use in place of a text decoder; our results locate it in the prompt rendering. We give a two-call test that tells a practitioner which case applies to their model, and measure what four mitigations are worth.

## Benchmarking System One decision models against trained classifiers and language models for automated decision gates
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00346v2 · published 2026-09-29
- Authors: Amir Rafe, Subasish Das

Software that hands branching decisions to a model needs a declared option and a probability it can threshold. Typed decision models, also called System One models, return such probabilities without generating text, while supervised classifiers and generative language models are the established alternatives. ## Prism-1: Position- and Rival-Independent Scoring for System One Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22066v2 · published 2026-10-15
- Authors: Yao Long Teng, Jianguo Huang, Jiayi Cai, Yanchen Deng, Bo An

Given a state and a set of options, such as intents, tools or interface elements, System One decision models return a distribution over the options in one forward pass, without generated reasoning. Web systems increasingly build them by reading decisions from language models. Two axioms of rational choice are natural requirements for such decisions, because in web systems the listing and the option set are determined by other components and can change or be manipulated: the order in which the options are listed should not matter, and adding an option should not change the relative odds of the others (Luce's choice axiom). We test the standard reads, namely single-choice question (SCQ) prompting, with shuffled-order training, order averaging or sorted options, and invariant set architectures, and find that each violates at least one axiom: one shuffle changes 12-20% of SCQ answers, sorting only freezes an arbitrary convention, and a well-chosen added option steers 4-34% of decisions. By Luce's theorem, a read satisfies both axioms only if it is a softmax over scores that each depend on one option alone, so in a transformer they hold by construction only if no token that scores an option sees another. We implement this as Prism-1 (Position- and Rival-Independent Scoring for System One Models): every option is read in its own block at shared positions, with the model's usual left-to-right attention, in one forward pass. On five benchmark sets with Qwen3-8B and Gemma 4 12B, SCQ prompting trained on the same data still changes 7% of its answers under one shuffle, and an added irrelevant option steers 12% of its decisions. Prism-1 stays within one point of that baseline's accuracy and is 2-4 points more accurate once only answers that survive a reordering are counted, while no shuffle changes any of its answers and no added irrelevant option moves its choice among the others.

## Sharp and Stable Label-Free Calibration Certificates from Logical Coherence
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21567v1 · published 2026-10-15
- Authors: Takahiro Kato

Query-conditioned probabilistic predictors answer logically related questions one at a time, and labels to check their calibration are often missing. For each restriction on what a bettor's stakes may use, a minimax theorem identifies, for finitely supported laws of the answers, the sharp label-free lower bound on the corresponding calibration residual. On bundles of different events its mean, marginal and joint stages can separate strictly, and a bundle's bound cannot be attributed to one question without assumptions on the others. The sharp marginal bound is discontinuous in the answers, and no label-free lower bound continuous in the Wasserstein distance stays within a constant factor of it. Lipschitz stakes give a stable bound, sharp for a smooth calibration residual and, for every positive Lipschitz constant, positive exactly when the sharp marginal bound is. On i.i.d. streams, projected online gradient ascent at fixed nodes gives fixed-horizon certificates whose shortfall is bounded by grid, learning and statistical terms, and predictable betting gives anytime-valid ones. In a preregistered study of four predictors on two data sets, all seven confirmatory certificates were positive (the eighth pair did not pass the pilot). A certificate shows only that calibration is at least this bad; zero means "not refuted".

## SO-Arena: Benchmarking the Capability Spectrum and Reliability of System One Models
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15777v1 · published 2026-11-11
- Authors: Lichen Li, Ziping Dong, Sicheng Feng, Qi Li, Xinchao Wang

System One models promise fast, general-purpose decision-making without lengthy token generation. However, understanding their capability spectrum and decision reliability requires more than aggregate performance on current benchmarks. Existing evaluations often provide limited insight into the breadth of model capabilities and the reliability of their decisions. We introduce SO-Arena, a benchmark characterizing the capability spectrum and decision reliability of System One models across 11 domains and five capability families. For the capability spectrum, SO-Arena offers evidence-grounded evaluation through reliable sources and manual verification of reference labels against model-visible inputs; fine-grained capability assessment through domain-specific difficulty criteria; and realistic interactive evaluation, where models iteratively execute actions in sandboxes. Reasoning-intensive tasks further enable system one-two comparison. Beyond accuracy, SO-Arena assesses decision reliability through two complementary measures: decision stability under controlled option-set changes and missing-answer recognition when no valid option is available. Evaluations across 21 models reveal that the benefits of scaling and additional reasoning vary substantially across domains. More importantly, strong overall performance does not necessarily imply reliable decision-making: the best-performing System One model ranks only seventh in reliability. Together, these findings highlight the need to characterize capability spectrum and decision reliability as complementary dimensions, providing a systematic view of the strengths and limitations of System One models.

## Evaluating and Benchmarking the System One Model Jev
- arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37647v2 · published 2026-09-29
- Authors: Tobias Deußer, Rajkumar Ramamurthy, Lorenz Sparrenberg, Rafet Sifa

Jev is a commercial "System One" model from TypeSafe AI that does not generate text: given a state and typed questions, it returns a choice from fixed options, a position on a rubric, or the probability that a statement is true, with probabilities described as calibrated. Such models target small decisions in information access pipelines. We evaluate Jev (jev-1.13.0) zero-shot on 37 datasets, from classification and routing to moderation and rubric scoring, with one frozen template per dataset and full evaluation splits: 346,009 requests for under US$10. For reference, we score Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma-4-E4B on identical requests via their exact option probabilities. Jev reaches 95-99% accuracy on IMDB, SST-2, HellaSwag and ARC, beats Qwen on 27 of 37 datasets, with none of Qwen's leads outside the bootstrap intervals, and Gemma on all 37, and its choice probabilities are well calibrated and support selective prediction. All three models degrade on low-resource languages, fine-grained or noisy labels, and rubric-based judgments, and binary probabilities need tuned thresholds, which raise micro-F1 on UNFAIR-ToS from 0.50 to 0.75. Jev answers MMLU's calculation-heavy questions more accurately than other MMLU questions, unlike both open models; probes rule out shallow memorization but not memorized question-answer pairs. Extending the protocol to sequential decisions, where the model chooses the next action at every step, Jev completes 35% of ALFWorld's unseen tasks, 42% of 90 TextWorld games and 21% of BabyAI-Text missions, chooses the optimal Blackjack action in 90% of decisions, and on FrozenLake almost never moves away from the goal to avoid holes. It often repeats actions that had no effect. Qwen completes more ALFWorld and TextWorld games than Jev (54.5% and 51.1%) but shares its failures on BabyAI-Text and FrozenLake. We release the code, harness and all raw responses.
